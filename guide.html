Give your collection a guardian

This catalog has been wiped out twice. Both times it came back within a day — not because anyone was careful, but because Claude had built a small safety system around it. None of it required writing code by hand. Here's the whole recipe, for records, books, watches, wine, or trading cards.

01Get the collection out of your head

Tell Claude what you collect and what you actually care about tracking — condition, year, what you paid, what it's worth now. It can turn that into a spreadsheet or a small private website like this one. If you already keep a messy list somewhere, hand that over; cleaning it up is exactly the kind of chore Claude is good at. Budget an afternoon, most of it spent typing in your items.

02Let it look up what things are worth

This is where the list becomes interesting. Ask Claude to set up a scheduled task — a saved instruction that runs on its own, say every Sunday — that checks marketplace prices (Discogs for records, eBay sold listings, whatever fits your hobby) and writes the going rates next to each item. This site's shelf is worth about €2,200 right now, and that number visibly moved when one pressing suddenly got popular.

Fair warning: the first few runs, a handful of items won't match cleanly and you'll need to point at the right ones. After that it mostly behaves.

03Back it up before you need to

One more scheduled task, this one nightly: export everything to a dated file in a safe place, like a free GitHub repository. That's it. When this catalog vanished the second time, recovery meant pasting in yesterday's file. Ten minutes, mildly annoying, nothing lost.

04Hire the watchdog

The piece that ties it together is a daily check that only reads, never touches. Every morning it counts the items, makes sure the prices are still there, confirms last night's backup actually happened, and compares the live catalog against that backup. On a good day it says one line and shuts up. On a bad day it says exactly what's missing and which file to restore from — but it never "fixes" anything itself. The human does the fixing; the watchdog does the noticing.

05Then let it grow

Once this skeleton runs, additions are cheap — each one is a short conversation. A monthly "what went up in value" note. An alert when something on your wantlist drops below a price. A digest for your insurance file. You'll know what you want after living with it for a few weeks.

06What I'd tell a friend starting out

Build it in the order above, because every step is useful even if you stop there. Keep anything automatic strictly read-only — an agent that can edit your data will eventually be the thing that breaks it. And don't aim for perfect on day one: this site lost its catalog twice before the watchdog existed, and hasn't lost anything quietly since.
